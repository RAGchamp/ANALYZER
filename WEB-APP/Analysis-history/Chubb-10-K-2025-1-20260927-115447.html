Chubb Limited: tax position analysis, FY2025 (Note 12)

Summary

  • Tax expense rose faster than profit. Tax expense was $2,422m in FY2025, up 33.4%, while pre-tax income rose 13.9% to $13,044m. The effective tax rate went from 15.8% to 18.6%. The main driver is Bermuda's new 15% corporate income tax, which took effect on 1 January 2025 (Note 12, p.175, p.177).
  • Earlier years were flattered by one-off Bermuda benefits. FY2023's rate of 5.4% and FY2024's 15.8% include benefits from the Bermuda law being enacted ($1,135m and $55m). Without them the rates were about 17.3% and 16.3%, so FY2025's 18.6% is the first "clean" rate under the new regime.
  • The deferred tax position moved from a small net asset to a net liability. It went from a net asset of $19m to a net liability of $(429)m. Deferred tax liabilities (DTLs) grew by $801m, mainly on deferred policy acquisition costs. This was partly offset by a $444m fall in the valuation allowance (Note 12, p.178).
  • Cash taxes are higher than current tax. Taxes paid were $2,211m against current tax of $2,085m (106%), and the company moved to a net current tax receivable. Uncertain tax positions are small and falling ($79m gross).
  • The main reconciliation issue is that deferred tax in the cash flow statement ($330m) differs from the note's deferred tax expense ($337m). The gap is small but unexplained, and it is larger in FY2023 ($40m).

  • FY2023: a $1,135m benefit when the Bermuda law was enacted cut the rate sharply. This matches the foreign deferred benefit of $(1,021)m. It most likely reflects deferred tax assets recognised on the transition, but the note does not say so explicitly (p.176–177).
  • FY2025: 2025 is the first year Bermuda profits are taxed. The reconciliation shows Bermuda adding $327m (2.5pp) as a rate difference against Switzerland (p.177).
  • Geographic mix: 98.6% of pre-tax income is foreign ($12,859m of $13,044m). Swiss domestic income is only $185m (p.176).

Deferred taxes (p.178–179)

  • Deferred policy acquisition costs (DAC): the DTL on DAC rose from $1,005m to $1,650m (+64%). This is much faster than the balance sheet DAC asset, which rose from $8,358m to $10,008m (+19.7%). The difference probably reflects Bermuda business coming into the tax net, but this is not disclosed.
  • Investments: they swung from a $512m DTA to a $127m DTL. The DTA on unrealized depreciation fell from $787m to $234m as unrealized losses narrowed (OCI unrealized appreciation +$2,655m, p.108).
  • Valuation allowance: it fell by $444m, even though a new $179m allowance was booked against unrealized capital losses. That implies roughly $623m of releases or other movements that are not explained. The FY2025 reconciliation has no separate valuation allowance line.
  • Loss reserve discount: the DTA rose from $1,746m to $2,073m (+18.7%), in line with growth in reserves.
  • Carry-forwards: net operating losses of $579m (expiring from 2026) and US capital losses of $52m (expiring from 2028).
  • Un-remitted foreign earnings: a DTL of $303m (up from $251m). The withholding tax that has not been provided is described as "material" but not practicable to compute (p.176).

Red flags & areas for follow-up

  1. Deferred tax in the cash flow does not match the note. The cash flow shows $330m, $96m and $(1,124)m against the note's $337m, $86m and $(1,084)m. Management should explain the $40m difference in FY2023.
  2. The change in net deferred tax is not explained. The net position swung by $448m, but deferred expense was only $337m. The DTA on unrealized depreciation fell by $553m, which should have gone through OCI tax, yet OCI tax is only $149m. A roll-forward of the deferred tax balances is needed.
  3. Valuation allowance releases are not disclosed. The allowance fell by $444m despite a new $179m allowance. The releases and their effect on the tax rate or OCI should be disclosed.
  4. FY2024 OCI tax has the wrong sign. It shows a tax expense on a pre-tax loss. Ask for the tax split by OCI component.
  5. "Other foreign jurisdictions" is large and unexplained. It adds $796m (6.1pp) with no breakdown.
  6. Prior-year reconciliations are not comparable. The Swiss rate baseline changed from about 19.7% to 7.8%, and 2024 and 2023 are not restated in the new format.
  7. Taxes paid by jurisdiction are missing for prior years. The FY2024 and FY2023 columns are blank (p.178). It is not clear whether this is an extraction gap or a transition exemption.
  8. Unprovided withholding tax is material but unquantified (p.176).
  9. Many tax years remain open. The US is open from 2014 and Spain from 2012, and a new IRS examination of 2019–2023 began in January 2026.
  10. The swing to net tax interest income of $(23)m looks linked to the settlements and should be confirmed as non-recurring.
